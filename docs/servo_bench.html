Words you will see

pulse (µs)
The only thing a servo understands. About 1000–2000 µs covers its range, and 1500 µs is roughly the middle.
q (rad or °)
A joint angle as the simulator and the policy see it.
calibration
The table that turns q into a pulse, one row per servo.
horn
The plastic arm pressed onto the servo shaft. Its teeth decide where the leg starts.
stall
The servo pushes against something and can't reach its target. It buzzes, gets hot and draws about 0.7 A. Back off right away.

Before you touch the real robot

  • A phone inclinometer app, or any protractor
  • A separate 5 V supply for the servos, rated 5 A or more (never the ESP32's USB)
  • ESP32 flashed with hardware/esp32_servo_bridge
  • Body level on the stand, all four feet in the air
  • With power off, each joint turned by hand once to make sure no cable catches
01 · CALIBRATION

Teach the software where the leg really is

The policy says "knee at 42°". The servo only understands "1620 µs". Calibration measures how to convert one into the other, separately for each of the 12 servos. Each servo needs its own numbers for three reasons: the horn can sit on any tooth of the shaft, servos differ slightly inside, and left and right legs turn in opposite directions.

IN SHORTFor each joint: put the leg in the standing pose and mark it. Turn it a known angle and mark again. Mark both ends just before it hits something. Then run the sweep and check the leg follows. Four marks per joint, twelve joints.

Calibration: measure the hardware

  • Neutral pulse for the reference pose (center_us)
  • Rotation sign (direction)
  • Pulse per radian (us_per_rad)
  • Safe physical range (min_us, max_us)

Motion control: use the range well

  • Speed and acceleration limits
  • Interpolation between poses
  • Foot trajectories and gaits
  • The trained policy itself
us = center_us + direction × us_per_rad × (q − zero_rad) then clamp to [min_us, max_us]
Joint on the bench
  1. Nudge the pulse until the leg reads the standing angle, then press Mark center.
  2. Turn the leg +30° further, in the direction shown below, then press Mark reference.
  3. Go toward one end. Stop a few degrees before it touches anything, then press Mark limit A.
  4. Do the same at the other end and press Mark limit B.

Center far from 1500 µs, or the sweep gets clamped? Re-seat the horn.

Moving the horn by one tooth shifts the whole leg by one tooth angle and clears this joint's marks. Start again at step 1. Aim for a standing pose near 1500 µs so the servo has room on both sides.

Now the simulator commands an angle, your calibration converts it to a pulse, and the real servo moves. The dashed leg is where the simulator thinks the leg is.

02 · POWER BUDGET

How much current do 12 servos need?

Multiply the servo count by the current per servo in each situation. Size the supply for the walking average with margin, and let a capacitor cover the short peaks. MG90S currents are not published by the maker; the defaults are typical figures, so replace them with numbers from an inline meter once you have one.

IN SHORTUse a 5 V supply rated 5–6 A for the servos. The numbers below show why. Once you own an inline current meter, replace the default currents with your own readings.

worst case = N × I_stall walking avg = N × I_moving supply ≥ max(walking avg × 1.5, worst case × 0.6) runtime (min) = battery Wh × UBEC efficiency ÷ (5 V × walking avg) × 60

03 · WIRES & CONNECTORS

Every wire and contact is a small resistor

Copper has resistance: R = ρ × L ÷ A. Thinner (higher AWG) and longer wire means more resistance. Current goes out on + and back on −, so use the round-trip length. Two checks matter: heat (ampacity) and voltage drop. On short runs, the connectors often matter more than the wire.

IN SHORTRun 18 AWG wire, 20 cm or shorter, from the supply to the PCA9685 V+ screw terminal. Keep Dupont jumpers and breadboards out of the servo power path. They get hot and drop the voltage.

ΔV = I × (R_wire + R_contacts) R_wire = Ω/m × (2 × one-way length) heat P = I² × R target: ΔV < 0.15 V (3% of 5 V)

04 · CAPACITOR

A small reservoir right next to the servos

When servos start moving, current jumps within a fraction of a millisecond. The supply and the wiring cannot follow instantly, so the 5 V rail dips. A large electrolytic capacitor at the PCA9685 V+ terminal supplies the first moments of each burst and refills between them. It covers short peaks only; it cannot replace a supply that is too small.

IN SHORTPut a 1000 µF (10 V or more) capacitor straight on the PCA9685 V+ terminal. The stripe marks the − leg. It stops the twitching and ESP32 resets you get when many servos start at once.

ΔV ≈ I × Δt ÷ C e.g. 4 A for 0.1 ms on 2200 µF → 0.18 V rule of thumb: about 100 µF per servo → 12 servos ≈ 1000–2200 µF, rated 10 V or more
